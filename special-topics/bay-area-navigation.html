Bay Area Navigation Networks

Organization-level risk analysis, a set of potential options for health-housing navigators to weigh, and the people and institutions shaping the response, as Medi-Cal work requirements (effective January 1, 2027 nationally) intersect with the region's dense but unevenly resourced safety-net infrastructure.

Throughline: Federal savings from OBBBA's Medicaid provisions are consistently smaller than the costs they shift onto county health systems, MCOs, and safety-net providers — and no reconciliation event has closed that gap yet. That downstream-cost multiplier is not uniform: the welfare-economics literature (Finkelstein, Hendren & Luttmer; CBO's own Medicaid analyses) generally finds Medicaid substitutes for uncompensated care hospitals would have provided anyway, so the average multiplier across the enrolled population is likely below 1:1 — but for the most medically complex and housing-unstable members (the externality table's ESRD, behavioral-health, and SUD rows below), downstream costs genuinely can run 3-5x the cost of managed coverage. Treat that range as describing high-utilizer subgroups, not the general Medi-Cal population. In the Bay Area specifically, that gap lands unevenly — dense urban cores with mature Medi-Cal MCO and FQHC infrastructure are better positioned to absorb documentation-driven disenrollment than the region's thinner safety-net edges.

Potential Actionable Insights for Navigation Networks

A curated set from a 20-issue navigator action framework spanning administrative/compliance, infrastructure, workforce, funding, market-structure, and clinical-integration problems — each paired with an option to weigh first, a fallback, and the incentive structure that would make it durable rather than one-off.

How to read this section: these are potential options, not recommendations or a plan of record. The intent is to explore what solution space exists and surface the trade-offs in each — not to predict or prescribe what any organization will actually do. Which of these stakeholders pursue, in what order, and whether they work at all, remains genuinely open: several depend on decisions (CalAIM waiver renewal, MCP contracting choices, litigation outcomes) that are not settled as of this writing.

Evidence standard. Every factual claim in the cards below is traceable to a named source you can open and check. The source framework these options came from carried supporting claims that were re-checked against primary sources, and a substantial share did not survive — those are not presented here as findings. They are listed in full, with the reason each failed, in What was excluded at the foot of this section. Nothing was quietly dropped, and nothing unverified is stated as fact.

Administrative

Procedural Medi-Cal disenrollment (work requirements + 6-month redeterminations)

Lead option to weighPrioritize automated ex parte verification of income and work status against existing state data (wage records, tax and motor-vehicle files) rather than asking members to report manually. Arkansas's 2018-19 programme — still the only completed U.S. Medicaid work requirement — is the relevant precedent: most of the subject population was cleared administratively, while the minority pushed into manual reporting accounted for the great majority of disenrollments (Health Affairs, Manatt). Nebraska's rollout has leaned on the same lever (KFF).
Who: DHCS, county social services agencies, CalHEERS vendors
Fallback optionMedical-legal partnerships at FQHCs as a secondary tool, focused on contested medical-frailty cases and appeals that automation cannot resolve. CMS's interim final rule sets the categorical exemptions — pregnant and postpartum people, Tribal members, veterans with a total disability rating, medically frail individuals, certain caregivers, and those already meeting SNAP/TANF work rules — and limits self-attestation to once per continuous enrollment period, after which documentation is required (CMS-2454-IFC). Housing instability is not itself a listed exemption, so unhoused clients route through the medically-frail pathway or a state-authorized hardship exception.
Who: La Clínica, TVHC, Bay Area Legal Aid, UC Berkeley & Stanford Law
Why it could hold — incentive leveragePeople who lose Medicaid for work-requirement noncompliance are not a Covered California fallback: ACA premium tax credits under §36B are unavailable to them. Navigator messaging should not imply otherwise. For hospitals, the cost-avoidance case is direct — California's LAO projects statewide uncompensated care rising from "a little over $2 billion" in 2024 by "as much as a few billion dollars annually by 2030," attributing nearly 90% of the underlying coverage loss to H.R.1 (LAO).

EvidenceDHCS puts 1.1M at risk of losing coverage by 2029-30 (4.8M subject, 2.2M expected exempt), attributing much of the expected loss to administrative burden rather than findings of ineligibility — CHCF, citing DHCS. In the 2023-24 unwinding, 69-70% of disenrollments were procedural rather than eligibility-based (KFF) and 47% of those disenrolled later re-enrolled (KFF) — the closest available measure of how much procedural loss falls on people who remained eligible.

Day-to-day impactDaily paperwork triage — verifying renewal deadlines, gathering income and address documentation, submitting renewals. Once 6-month redeterminations begin, that becomes a recurring calendar alert per client rather than an annual one.

Infrastructure

Navigator system fragmentation — 3-5 non-interoperable platforms per client (EHR, HMIS, CalHEERS, CalSAWS, Salesforce)

Lead option to weighRoute through California's Data Exchange Framework and DHCS-governed CalAIM data sharing (DHCS) rather than building a bespoke integration. CalHEERS and CalSAWS are state and county eligibility systems; a community organization should not plan on direct API access to either. A shared case-management layer can still standardize an org's own intake, but it is not a bridge into those two systems.
Who: DHCS Data Exchange Framework team, county HMIS admins, CHCN
Fallback optionWhere a shared org-level layer is feasible, standardize data entry to cut duplicate documentation and use managed-care-plan member renewal-date files as the redetermination signal.
Who: Abode Services, Hamilton Families, BACS, La Clínica
Why it could hold — incentive leverageAny data-sharing design has to map consent under HIPAA, HUD's HMIS rules (24 CFR Part 576), and 42 CFR Part 2 for substance-use records before systems are linked — that mapping is a prerequisite, not an afterthought, and is the main reason these integrations stall.

EvidenceThe best-established measure of documentation burden in healthcare is physician-based: Sinsky et al. found 49.2% of physician time went to EHR and desk work against 27% in direct face time (Annals of Internal Medicine, 2016). It is cited here as the nearest rigorous analogue, not as a navigator measurement — see What was excluded on the absence of navigator-specific data. CHCF convening materials document providers feeling overwhelmed by CalAIM's pace (CHCF).

Day-to-day impactHousing navigators enter each client contact into HMIS, update Coordinated Entry status, log it in a program CRM, then report monthly in a county system. Health navigators document in an EHR plus a Medi-Cal portal plus a program system. One client interaction can mean four separate data entries.

Workforce

CHW/navigator retention under Bay Area cost of living — turnover destroys institutional knowledge

Lead option to weighWhere CHW services meet the billing criteria an org's Medi-Cal managed care plan sets under CalAIM, route staff time into the state's CHW benefit so it becomes billable rather than pure grant cost. The benefit took effect July 1, 2022 under AB 133 (Ch. 143, Statutes of 2021), with SB 184 setting core competencies (DHCS, NASHP). Note DHCS certification and MCP billing eligibility are separate processes, and FQHC-specific billing rules for CHW visits are still developing — confirm both before budgeting on the revenue.
Who: FQHCs, Abode, Hamilton Families, BACS
Fallback optionPool a regional CHW retention fund across foundations for salary supplements independent of any single org's budget cycle. CHCF's own evaluation found financial sustainability remains the central challenge for CHW programmes even after the billable benefit existed (CHCF).
Who: DHCS, regional community foundations
Why it could hold — incentive leverageWhere billing criteria are met, certified CHW time becomes a revenue-generating category rather than a grant cost, and plans benefit from the resulting drop in avoidable utilization. Before treating this as a funding strategy, run the break-even explicitly: billable units × the Medi-Cal CHW rate against the fully-loaded salary — which in this region is higher than commonly assumed.

EvidenceBLS puts the mean community health worker wage in the SF-Oakland-Hayward metro at $69,030, median $32.15/hour across 1,710 employed (BLS OEWS). That is materially above the range the source framework assumed, which shifts the argument: the retention problem here rests on Bay Area cost of living, not on unusually low pay. CHCF separately reports most organizations struggle with CHW recruitment and retention (CHCF).

Day-to-day impactA CHW day: 25-50 active clients; morning triage of voicemails and texts; checking for overnight ED visits; chasing renewal deadlines; field visits at food banks, shelters and encampments; a 60-90 minute first intake; referral management; documentation. Which clinic has a two-week versus six-month wait lives in their head — and leaves with them.

Funding

No flex/discretionary funds for navigators — a structural gap that blocks otherwise-completable placements

Lead option to weighLead with CalAIM Housing Deposits, a Community Support, as the vehicle for one-time move-in costs — a Medi-Cal-billable rail rather than a discretionary fund dependent on grant cycles (DHCS). Use org reserves only as a residual for costs it does not cover. One barrier often cited in this context does not apply in California: the DMV waives the state ID fee for people experiencing homelessness (CA DMV).
Who: Medi-Cal MCPs (CalAIM Housing Deposits), org operating reserves
Fallback optionCounty emergency-assistance funds with a short turnaround for navigator requests, and pre-positioned stored-value cards for costs outside CalAIM's covered categories.
Who: County human services departments, local emergency funds
Why it could hold — incentive leverageThe structural argument does not depend on a leverage ratio: a covered Community Support converts an unpredictable, grant-funded expense into a billable one, which is what makes it durable. Any specific multiple of return on flex spending should be treated as illustrative unless an org measures its own.

EvidenceCalAIM Community Supports explicitly include housing deposits, housing transition navigation, and tenancy sustaining services as Medi-Cal-billable categories when a plan elects and contracts them (DHCS). The claim that frontline staff routinely subsidise client costs personally is discussed under What was excluded — the available survey evidence is real but not transferable to this setting.

Day-to-day impactA placement stalls on something small — a document fee, a bus pass to reach the appointment, a storage deposit — and program rules bar spending on it. With a flex line it resolves in minutes; without one it becomes a multi-week delay and another case cycling back.

Market Structure

Safety-net FQHC acquisition risk by non-mission-aligned entities, triggered by fiscal distress

Lead option to weighEnsure a mission-aligned nonprofit acquirer wins any FQHC acquisition — which is the only durable protection, since FQHC status requires nonprofit or public-agency status under HRSA Section 330 and cannot be preserved by a for-profit acquirer on any deal terms (HRSA). Build a distress-monitoring score from UDS and Form 990 liquidity data to flag at-risk orgs early, and use California's OHCA material-change transaction review as the pre-close leverage point (OHCA).
Who: OHCA, State AG healthcare rights units, county health officers, CHCF/SVCF as grantors
Fallback optionCDFI lenders pre-position bridge or acquisition financing for mission-aligned consolidators, so that a capable nonprofit buyer is not excluded simply by how long its financing takes to assemble.
Who: Community development financial institutions (CDFIs)
Why it could hold — incentive leverage340B savings are a significant margin source for health centres and give a mission-aligned acquirer a real reason to preserve the designation — though the value concentrates in commercial and Medicare volume, since Medicaid is typically carved out or reimbursed near acquisition cost. That makes the incentive strongest for centres with a mixed payer base, and weakest for the most Medi-Cal-dependent ones.

EvidenceFQHC designation carries four distinct benefits that all lapse together if a centre is absorbed by a non-FQHC entity: the federal Section 330 grant, Medi-Cal cost-based reimbursement, 340B drug pricing, and NHSC workforce eligibility (HRSA). The comparative acquisition-timing claim the source framework used is addressed under What was excluded.

Day-to-day impactNo direct daily change — this one is felt afterwards. Staff at an FQHC absorbed by a mission-aligned nonprofit keep the patient-majority governing board; staff at one absorbed by a non-FQHC entity see that accountability structure dissolve and service lines reweighted toward commercially profitable care.

Clinical Integration

Health and housing navigators operate in parallel silos — CalAIM requires an integration most orgs haven't built yet

Lead option to weighPrioritize the Community Supports delivered as In-Lieu-of-Services, since ILOS billing does not depend on 1115 waiver authority — the more durable rail given that CalAIM's waiver expires December 31, 2026, with the 2027-2031 renewal submitted to CMS in May 2026 and approval expected only in late December, at the cliff edge (Aurrera Health). Co-located, co-supervised teams still improve case quality, but billing is not contingent on co-location: each plan elects which Community Supports to offer independently of any org's structure.
Who: Abode Services, BACS, Hamilton Families, La Clínica
Fallback optionWhere co-location isn't feasible, a standardized warm-handoff referral protocol between health and housing orgs rather than paper referrals.
Who: Regional homelessness-coordination bodies, CHCN
Why it could hold — incentive leverageHousing supports have measurable utilization effects that plans can act on, and the services are already a billable category — so the case does not rest on goodwill. Track the December 2026 renewal decision closely: it determines whether waiver-dependent, non-ILOS supports continue at all.

EvidenceDHCS's own Housing Trio results, measured over the six months following use: inpatient use −24.3%, ED use −13.2%, and applicable service costs −31.6% for Housing Deposits (DHCS). Separately, homeless patients' hospital readmission rate is 27.3% against 17.5% for housed patients, adjusted OR 1.93 (PubMed).

Day-to-day impactIn an integrated team the housing navigator and CHW share a desk and a 15-minute morning huddle flagging which shared clients had an issue overnight. The CHW's note on a health barrier is visible to the housing navigator in the application context. One conversation catches what would otherwise be two notes that never meet.

What was excluded, and why

The source framework attached supporting claims to each of these options. Re-checked against primary sources, 6 of 13 did not hold. They are listed here rather than in the cards, because material that failed verification should not sit alongside material that passed as though the two were equivalent.

Wrong — not used
  • "77% of those who lost Medicaid during redetermination still qualified." A misattribution: KFF's 77% is a satisfaction figure — the share of pre-unwinding enrollees rating their Medicaid experience excellent or good. It says nothing about eligibility. The administrative-disenrollment card instead uses the two figures that do bear on the question: 69-70% of unwinding disenrollments were procedural, and 47% of those disenrolled later re-enrolled.
  • "Private equity can close in 30-60 days; nonprofit acquirers need 6-12 months." Untraceable in either direction, and probably inverted: a 30-60 day figure does recur in this literature, but it describes the state regulatory notice period required before closing a healthcare transaction (Health Affairs Forefront) — a constraint on speed, not evidence of it. The FQHC card's argument for pre-positioned financing stands on its own without the comparison.
Corrected before use
  • DHCS coverage-loss figure: 1.4M → 1.1M. No defensible 1.4M DHCS figure could be located; 1.1M is the agency's central estimate.
  • CHW wage: $45-60K → $69,030. BLS OEWS for the SF-Oakland-Hayward metro. This cuts against the framework's own use of the number, so the card's argument was rewritten around cost of living rather than low pay.
  • CHW billing authority: AB 2468 → AB 133 (2021). AB 2468 is not the enabling statute; the benefit took effect July 1, 2022, with SB 184 setting core competencies.
  • Housing supports: "ED −20-35%, readmissions −27%" → inpatient −24.3%, ED −13.2%. The original overstated the ED effect by roughly double. A larger 71%/24% pair in circulation comes from an LA County psychiatric recuperative care service, not housing supports generally — the likely origin of the inflation.
Untraceable — not stated as fact anywhere in the cards
  • "Navigator time-use studies consistently report 30-40% of hours on documentation." No navigator, CHW, or case-manager study supports this; the one CHW time-use study located (rural Tanzania) puts documentation at 7.8%, which is not comparable. The infrastructure card cites the physician literature instead, labelled as an analogue. The absence of navigator-specific time-use data is itself a finding worth naming.
  • "Turnover costs $8,000-15,000 per hire." Not traceable to a CHW-specific study, and comparable estimates bracket it incompatibly — roughly $2,100-4,200 per instance for direct care workers, against $35-50K under percentage-of-salary methods for a $69K role. No figure is asserted.
  • "Unmet basic needs are the most common preventable barrier to housing placement completion." No source located. The underlying observation may well be right; the superlative is what makes it unpublishable.
  • "Many navigators spend personal money on client needs." The closest evidence is a BASW membership survey in which about 70% of 290 social workers reported using their own money or resources for clients (via Community Care) — UK social workers rather than US navigators, and a membership survey rather than peer-reviewed research. Not transferable enough to carry an argument here.

Scope. The framework carries 20 issues; this page carries 6. The 13 claims audited are those attached to the 6 published cards. The remaining 14 items carry their own supporting claims that have not been checked — given that roughly half of this sample failed, they should not be assumed sound if any are promoted here later.

County-Level Risk Delineation

The same disenrollment mechanics translate into different effective risk depending on each county's safety-net density, fiscal position, and geography. Alameda and Santa Clara figures below are sourced from county officials and UC Berkeley Labor Center's county-level projections; the remaining three counties are directionally modeled estimates, not confirmed counts — treat those as lower-confidence.

Alameda County

14,600 by 2026-27

County social services projects 14,600 of the ~159,000 residents subject to the work requirement lose coverage in 2026-27, rising to 50,000+ by 2030 (total Medi-Cal enrollment ~400,000). AHS is the sole safety-net trauma center; 8 FQHCs in the county's health-center consortium serve 260,000+ patients, exposed to varying degrees. Highest-risk cities: Oakland (all districts), Hayward, Fremont, Union City, Livermore, Emeryville.

Santa Clara County

129,000 by 2028

UC Berkeley Labor Center projects 129,000 residents losing Medi-Cal by 2028, against ~460,000 total enrolled (the county's ~500,000 Medi-Cal figure elsewhere is total enrollment, not the projected loss). A county-wide fiscal deficit (recently offset in part by a dedicated local sales-tax measure directed to the county health system) forces service triage. SCVMC is the only Level I trauma center in San Jose and one of four Northern California burn centers. Highest-risk areas: East San Jose, Gilroy (agricultural workers), North San Jose (warehouse workers).

San Francisco County

~120,000-150,000 [est., unverified]

ZSFG is the sole public hospital and trauma center; SF has among the highest per-capita unhoused populations in California, creating a convergence of housing and health risk. Highest-risk neighborhoods: Tenderloin, SoMa, Bayview-Hunters Point, Mission. This county's coverage-loss figure was not independently corroborated against an official source in this research pass — treat as directional only.

Contra Costa County

~100,000-130,000 [est., unverified]

La Clínica's Concord/Antioch sites and LifeLong's Richmond site face the same coverage cliff as Alameda County sites; Contra Costa Health Services is the county safety net but has less capacity than AHS. Highest-risk cities: Richmond, Antioch, Concord, Pittsburg. Not independently corroborated against an official source.

San Mateo County

~60,000-80,000 [est., unverified]

Relative county-wide affluence means a lower overall Medi-Cal base, but the immigrant-worker population in Daly City, Redwood City, and South County is substantial with limited FQHC backup. Abode Services operates permanent supportive housing here. Not independently corroborated against an official source.

Methodology, Sourcing & Limitations

Where this page's content comes from

  • Organization risk rankings, county-level figures, and the externality cost reference are drawn from a prior detailed org-risk-analysis working document prepared for this project, itself modeled from ProPublica Form 990 filings (most recent available), regional healthcare market reports, county-level fiscal analyses, and peer-reviewed externality-cost literature. Where exact 990 figures were unavailable, estimates are labeled [est.] and modeled from sector averages.
  • The actionable-insights framework is a curated subset of a 20-issue navigator action framework covering administrative, infrastructure, workforce, funding, market-structure, and clinical-integration problems, each originally scored against real navigator/case-manager daily-work impact.
  • Every dollar figure on this page is a planning estimate, not an audited projection — actual outcomes depend on state mitigation, litigation results, and org-level response between now and 2028.
  • Estimate policy: lead conservative. Where a range exists, read the low end. Where an official agency figure and a modeled advocacy figure disagree, this page uses the agency's own central estimate and names the wider figure separately rather than headlining it (e.g. Montana's ~13,000 from DPHHS rather than the 10,000-30,000 span in circulation; California's ~1.1M DHCS central estimate rather than the 3.4M high-end scenario). Where no org- or county-specific figure is traceable to a named source, the page states that rather than deriving one from a statewide average.

What was independently re-verified before publishing

  • Confirmed National Medicaid work-requirement effective date is January 1, 2027 (CMS interim final rule, June 2026) — not "2028," which appeared in an earlier working draft of this analysis and has been corrected throughout this page.
  • Confirmed Named litigation (Commonwealth of Massachusetts et al. v. Oz), lead plaintiff, and the July 29, 2026 preliminary-injunction denial — see the main Special Topics tab for full detail and sourcing.
  • Modeled / not individually re-verified Organization-level dollar figures, patient counts, and externality-cost ranges — these were sourced from the underlying working document's own citations (ProPublica 990s, CHCF regional reports, UC Berkeley Labor Center county projections, peer-reviewed cost literature) but were not re-run against primary sources one by one in this pass. Treat every $ figure and patient count on this page as a planning-level estimate.
  • Not independently verified Named county-level officials' specific quoted framing and some named research/advocacy voices from the underlying working document were not carried onto this page pending direct source verification — the People & Entities table above lists only roles independently corroborated elsewhere in this project's research.

How the resilient/at-risk rankings were built

  • Organizations were selected as real, currently operating Bay Area entities in categories directly relevant to Medi-Cal work-requirement exposure (health systems, Medi-Cal MCOs, FQHCs, safety-net hospitals, housing/behavioral-health nonprofits, and funders).
  • Ranking is a structural assessment — payer-mix diversification, known infrastructure maturity (e.g. CalAIM/1115-waiver systems), reserve position, and organization scale — not an audited financial or credit-rating analysis. No individual organization's balance sheet, reserve position, or bond rating was independently verified for this page.
  • Revenue-at-risk methodology (from the source working document): estimated revenue × Medi-Cal share × a population-specific disenrollment-rate assumption (immigrant-heavy populations 35-50%; general low-income 15-25%; homeless-adjacent 20-30%) — a planning heuristic, not a statistical model validated against realized outcomes. This methodology likely overstates exposure: it applies the disenrollment rate to an org's entire Medi-Cal revenue, but only the ACA expansion adult population (not children, seniors, pregnant enrollees, disabled enrollees, or dual-eligibles) is subject to the work requirement — DHCS puts that population at ~34% of total Medi-Cal enrollment statewide (4.8M of ~14M), with a central coverage-loss estimate of ~1.1M (roughly 7-8% of enrollment), not the much larger share these dollar figures could be read to imply. Orgs with heavier elderly, disabled, or pediatric caseloads are more insulated than their flat Medi-Cal % suggests; Alameda County's own official figure (14,600 of 159,000 subject = ~9% losing coverage in 2026-27) is a more grounded near-term reference point than this page's org-level ranges.
